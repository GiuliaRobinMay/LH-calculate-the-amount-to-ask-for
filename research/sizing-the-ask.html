How much should you ask for?

Research brief for the Calculate My Funding Amount tool in the Lesko Toolbox: what the competition offers, what grant professionals and funders actually advise, the real program numbers, and the question set that follows from all of it.

7 September 2026 · prepared for Giulia and the Lesko Help team

The finding that shapes the tool

Nobody has built this — and the honest answer is a method, not a number.

No consumer tool anywhere helps a person decide the amount. Benefit screeners tell you if you qualify. Nonprofit software tells organizations to read a funder's tax return. Business-grant sites fix the amount for you. The professionals all teach the same thing: add up the real cost from real bills, subtract what is already covered, and ask for the gap — inside what that door actually gives. That is a worksheet. It fits the Lesko pattern exactly: private, on the device, no account.

The design decision

Two kinds of money, two different questions

Members ask "how much should I ask for?" about two very different things. The research says they need different worksheets, so the tool must sort them on page one.

Kind one · most members

Help with a cost I already have

Rent, a shut-off notice, a car repair, a medical bill, a funeral, a deposit, a roof. Here the program sets the number, not the member. Rent help pays up to a set number of months. A charity gives $200–$600 once a year. FEMA's average is $3,446.

"They prefer to be the last money in."

So the tool's job is: total the real cost, subtract what is covered, show the gap, then show what one door typically gives — and how many doors that means.

Kind two · Lesko Pro

Money for a business, nonprofit or project

Here the member sets the number and a reviewer judges it. The rules are professional: itemize from quotes, no round guesses, no "miscellaneous", never ask for 100% of the project, stay inside the funder's usual range, expect less the first time.

"If it's too large or too small, it won't do."

So the tool's job is: build a line-item budget with a reason for each line, compare the ask to the funder's typical award, and flag what a reviewer would flag.

What this is not: a program directory, a phone list, or a script. Per the product rule that governs every Lesko tool, it does one thing — turns the member's own numbers into one clear amount with the reasons behind it — and hands the rest to the community: the Quick Guides, the Questions Channel, the Call Sheet Classes.

The landscape

What the competition gives their people

Every adjacent tool either fixes the amount for you, estimates a statutory benefit, or tells nonprofits to read tax returns. None asks the member what things cost.

The direct competitor

Skip (helloskip.com)

Grants marketplace plus an AI writing assistant. Its own grants are fixed at $1K, $5K and $10K. The application vault covers six standard questions and "break down how the funds will drive growth." No amount help at all.

Pro nonprofit suites

Candid · Instrumentl · Grantable · Granted AI

Candid publishes a percentage-of-budget matrix (ask 40–60% of a project from a new funder, 70–90% from an existing one). Instrumentl profiles a funder's past awards from its 990-PF. Grantable and Granted AI take the budget as an input. Built for development officers, priced $20–$500 a month.

Small-business grant sites

Hello Alice · IFundWomen · Amber · Nav · FedEx

The sponsor fixes the award: Hello Alice mostly flat $20,000; Amber $10,000 monthly; Comcast RISE $5,000; FedEx $10,000. Applicants write a "use of funds" answer scored on "clarity and feasibility of the plan" (30%) and "demonstrated need" (40%). The number is judged, never coached.

Benefit screeners

findhelp · mRelief · BenefitsCheckUp · USA.gov

Eligibility only. mRelief screens SNAP in three minutes; BenefitsCheckUp returns a list of programs you "may get help from." The only tools that output a dollar figure are SNAP and Pell estimators, because those benefits are formulas. Nothing about what you should request.

Also checked: Grants.gov and SAM.gov (federal money "is not direct assistance to individuals"; only SF-424A budget forms and budget-narrative rules), GrantWatch and GrantStation (advice articles and an annual survey), and every "grant budget calculator" search result — which turns up Pell estimators, a calculator for funders, and generic AI line-item generators. The whitespace is real.

The method

What the professionals actually teach

Eight rules, each one quoted from a funder, a grant-training body or a federal regulation. Every question in the tool traces back to one of these.

  1. 1

    Ask for what it really costs — from a bill or a written quote

    The number comes from paper, not from the air. A round figure with no math behind it reads as a guess and costs credibility.

    "Costs should be based on actual quotes and/or estimates to ensure that they aren't padded or underestimated."

    National Council on Aging, budget justification guidance · Grantboost: "'$10,000 for supplies' reads as a guess… show units and unit costs."

  2. 2

    Never lowball, never pad

    The Grantsmanship Center calls it the Goldilocks rule. Asking for less than you need to look modest sets the project up to fail; padding distorts what the work costs.

    "If it's too large or too small, it won't do… an unrealistic budget in either direction will kill the credibility of your proposal."

    The Grantsmanship Center · MonkeyPod: "Never, ever lowball your budget—but don't pad it either." · Grantsights: funders "universally reject" a 'miscellaneous' or 'contingency' line.

  3. 3

    Subtract what is already covered — they want to be the last money in

    Charities, churches and disaster programs pay the gap, not the bill. After a disaster the order is fixed by law: insurance first, then FEMA, then an SBA loan — FEMA "cannot pay for that same loss again."

    "If you owe $1,000 and they can give $300, they will not give you a check until you prove you have paid the other $700."

    Salvation Army rent-assistance practice · FEMA, "Understanding Duplication of Benefits" · CRS report R45238.

  4. 4

    One-time money does not pay ongoing bills

    Emergency aid is built for a one-off shortfall. Federal TANF emergency help "will not extend beyond four months." Reviewers of business and nonprofit asks flag wages and rent as "we'll worry about that when the grant ends."

    Non-recurrent short-term benefits are "not intended to meet recurrent or ongoing needs."

    Administration for Children and Families, TANF policy guidance · Bloom Grant Consulting on sustainability flags.

  5. 5

    Stay inside what that door actually gives

    Every program has a ceiling and a habit. Asking a $5,000 funder for $25,000 is a red flag; asking rent help for a year when it caps at three months wastes a turn.

    "If a foundation typically gives between $25,000 and $50,000 per grantee, it's unlikely that they will suddenly award $200,000."

    GrantWatch · Candid: "if a foundation's top grant is $5,000, requesting $25,000 raises a red flag."

  6. 6

    First-timers get less — and most doors open once a year

    Instrumentl's example: median first award $15,000, median repeat award over $150,000. Most charities help once per 12 months, so an ask that is too small means a year's wait.

    "Funders may want to test the relationship first and give lower amounts to first-time grant awardees."

    Candid · Instrumentl · Salvation Army and Catholic Charities frequency rules ("once in a 12-month period").

  7. 7

    Count the costs people forget

    Deposits, first and last month, application fees, utility hook-ups, moving, the taxes on a business grant. Rent help can cover "security deposit, monthly rent, and utilities" — but only if you ask.

    A member, in the community: "$8,100–$9,300 upfront prior to moving in and that doesn't include… cleaning fee, pets… applications are $85–$120 per person."

    FEMA rental assistance fact sheet · USU Extension on grant taxation · Lesko Help member post, Washington State.

  8. 8

    Never ask for 100% of a project

    For business and nonprofit asks only. Show your own share, or in-kind help, so the reviewer sees the project can stand without them.

    "Avoid asking for 100% of project costs and show that your program can stand beyond one grant."

    Candid · 2 CFR 200.306 on cost sharing and in-kind contributions.

The mistakes

How first-timers get the amount wrong

Documented, not assumed. Each one becomes a flag the tool raises before the member sends anything.

A round number with nothing behind it"$5,000" with no bill. Fix: every line asks where the number came from.
Asking for the whole billCharities pay the gap. Fix: subtract what is covered before showing the ask.
Asking for rent foreverOne-time money for a monthly hole. Fix: separate one-time from monthly; count the months.
Asking outside the ceiling$25,000 from a $5,000 funder. Fix: compare the ask to the door's cap and typical award.
Forgetting the extrasDeposit, fees, hook-ups, taxes. Fix: a checklist of related costs that adds lines.
Stacking after a disasterFEMA and insurance for the same loss. Fix: ask what insurance and FEMA already paid, in that order.
A "miscellaneous" lineReviewers reject it. Fix: the tool won't accept a line without a name.
Asking too little from a once-a-year doorThen waiting twelve months. Fix: show the frequency rule next to the amount.
The proposal

The quiz, page by page

Same two-round shape as "What Should I Ask For?": one sorting page, then only the pages that apply. Every page carries the rule it comes from. Short, plain, no account.

Before building

Four decisions that are yours

Each one changes the build materially, so none is assumed.

1 · One path or both?

Path one (a cost I already have) serves most of the 22,000 members. Path two (business and nonprofit) serves Lesko Pro and is a bigger build with more rules.

Recommendation: both, with path one first and complete; path two ships in the same tool because the sorting page needs it.

2 · Show the real program numbers inside the tool?

The table above lets the result say "that is roughly four doors." It is powerful and sourced — but the figures age, and every one needs a "last checked" date and someone to refresh them each year.

Recommendation: yes, as typical ranges with the date and source shown, never as a promise — the brand rule on amounts.

3 · Members-only, or a public taste?

The space "Calculate My Funding Amount" already exists in the Lesko Toolbox (its page is switched off for now). The App Lab roadmap listed this as a free tool.

Recommendation: members-only inside the Toolbox first, same as the quiz; a public version is a later decision.

4 · The name

The space is titled "Calculate My Funding Amount". Next to "What Should I Ask For?" the natural sister name is "How Much Should I Ask For?" — the question members actually type.

Recommendation: "How Much Should I Ask For?" in the tool; keep or rename the space to match.

Assumed unless you say otherwise: same stack and pattern as the quiz (one HTML file, vanilla JS, on-device storage, no sign-up, no SSN or bank data, iframe embed in the Mighty space); state asked once, optionally, because heating help and rent help vary by state; "the quiz" wording; results link to guides and the Questions Channel only.
